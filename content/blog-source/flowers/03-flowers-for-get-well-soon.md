# Flowers for Get Well Soon: What to Send (and What to Avoid)

A get well soon gift has one job the others don't: it has to work in a room where the recipient may be sensitive to smell, low on energy, or simply not in a mood for anything too bold. That rules out a lot of what makes flowers exciting for other occasions — strong fragrance, bright saturated color, dramatic size — and points toward something gentler instead.

## Why Peace Lilies Are the Go-To Choice

Peace lilies come up more than almost any other option for recovery gifts, for a few practical reasons rather than just tradition. Their white blooms read as calm and hopeful without tipping into anything somber, which matters for a gift meant to reassure rather than dampen the mood further. They're also gentle in a literal sense — unlike heavily fragrant flowers that can be overwhelming in a hospital room or sickroom, a peace lily is unobtrusive, sitting quietly in the space rather than filling it.

There's a genuine practical benefit too: peace lilies actively help purify indoor air, which is a real plus in a recovery environment where the person may be spending most of their time in one room for days or weeks.

## Cut Flowers vs. a Living Plant: Which Says More

Cut flower bouquets work well for a short hospital stay — something bright to look at for the few days someone's admitted, with no expectation that they'll need to be cared for afterward. But for a longer recovery, especially one that continues at home, a plant has an advantage a bouquet doesn't: it doesn't have an expiry date tied to the illness. A bouquet wilts around the same time the recipient is (hopefully) starting to feel better; a plant is still there weeks later, on a windowsill, as something to tend rather than something that reminds them they were unwell.

A combination works well too — a smaller bouquet for the room now, alongside a peace lily or similar low-maintenance plant that outlasts the stay.

## What to Avoid When Sending Get Well Flowers

**Strong fragrance.** Lilies (in general, beyond peace lilies specifically), certain rose varieties, and heavily scented seasonal blooms can be genuinely unpleasant in an enclosed hospital room, especially for someone dealing with nausea or respiratory issues. Gentle, low-scent options are the safer default unless you know the recipient specifically loves a stronger fragrance.

**Anything with pollen.** Some hospitals restrict flowers with heavy pollen in certain wards (oncology and ICU in particular) due to infection risk. If you're unsure whether flowers are allowed in the recipient's specific ward, it's worth checking with a family member before ordering, or defaulting to a plant, which doesn't carry the same restriction in most cases.

**Overly festive or bright arrangements.** A get well gift isn't a birthday gift. Loud, celebratory color combinations can feel mismatched to the moment. Softer palettes — whites, pale pinks, gentle greens — tend to land better.

**Waiting too long to send it.** Recovery gifts matter most early — in the first few days, when the person is likely to feel most low. A same-day delivery, even a modest one, is generally better timed than a more elaborate gift that arrives after the worst of it has passed.

## A Simple Way to Decide

If you know the person will be in the hospital only briefly, a gentle cut-flower bouquet is enough. If the recovery is longer, or continues at home, lean toward a peace lily or a similar plant, either alone or paired with a small bouquet for the interim. Either way, keep it soft — fragrance-light, pale-colored, and unfussy.

## Quick Answers

**Are flowers allowed in all hospital rooms?**
Not always. Some wards, particularly ICU and oncology, restrict flowers due to infection control or pollen sensitivity. It's worth checking with the patient's family before ordering, or choosing a plant instead, which is generally allowed even where cut flowers aren't.

**What's the best plant to send someone recovering from illness?**
Peace lilies are a common choice for recovery gifts — their white blooms are calming rather than somber, they're low-fragrance, and they help purify indoor air, which is a genuine benefit in a room where someone is spending extended time.

**Should I send flowers or a plant for a hospital stay?**
For a short stay, cut flowers are usually enough. For a longer recovery, especially one continuing at home, a plant lasts beyond the illness itself and doesn't need to be replaced once it fades.

**Is it okay to send strongly scented flowers to someone who's unwell?**
Generally, no — strong fragrance can be uncomfortable in an enclosed sickroom, particularly for anyone dealing with nausea or respiratory symptoms. Gentle, low-scent flowers or a peace lily are safer defaults.
